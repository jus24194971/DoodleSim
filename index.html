🚀 Quick start — two ways to begin

I know what I need to accomplish

  1. Click 🎯 Plan Advisor
  2. Describe the mission: what talks to what, how far, how much data
  3. Review the ranked configurations and hit Apply to map
  4. The design lands on the map, aimed and cabled — verify it over your real deployment terrain

I want to build it myself

  1. + Add Node, then click the map to place each radio
  2. Configure each node in the sidebar (radio, band, antenna, height…)
  3. ⟋ Link Nodes, then click two nodes to analyze the path
  4. Click any link (on the map or sidebar) to open its terrain profile

📡 Configuring a node

Each node is a full radio install. The fields that matter most:

  • Platform — sets a realistic default height: mast 10 m, UAV 100 m, UGV 1 m, vessel 4 m, handheld 1.5 m. Height drives the terrain math, so keep it honest.
  • Radio & band — all current Mesh Rider models with their real per-MCS transmit power and sensitivity (the same conservative numbers as the official Doodle Labs estimator). Both ends of a link must share a band to talk.
  • Antenna — pick from the catalog (★ = Doodle Labs recommended; the rest are datasheet-verified third-party models) or enter a custom gain + beamwidth. Directional antennas get a beam wedge on the map.
  • Cable — choose MC400/MC600-class and a length; loss is computed automatically for the operating frequency (and updates if you change bands). Use "Manual" to type a known loss.
  • Fade margin (top right) — the dB of headroom every prediction must keep above receiver sensitivity. Doodle Labs plan to 10 dB; field engineers often work to 12–14 dB. Raise it for congested, maritime or contested RF; drop it only for bench work, and never quote a 0 dB figure to a customer. Every link, heatmap, route and advisor result uses the value you set, and the exported report states it.
  • BDA gain — add amplification (e.g. Mesh Rider Boost, Triad) as extra dB in the budget.

🎯 Aiming directional antennas

Sectors, dishes, yagis and panels show a blue beam wedge covering their real beamwidth. Drag the white handle at the wedge tip to aim — links and heatmaps recompute on release. Off-boresight targets pay a pattern penalty in both azimuth and elevation: a high-gain omni has a narrow vertical beam and will genuinely lose a drone directly overhead, and the Tilt field matters when serving targets far above or below you.

🗺 Reading the map

  • Green link — clear line of sight with a healthy margin
  • Amber link — Fresnel-zone intrusion: it may work, expect degradation in rain/multipath
  • Orange link — terrain blocks the direct ray; running on diffraction
  • Red link — the budget doesn't close at any data rate

Every link label shows distance, the best usable MCS, and expected throughput. Click a link for the terrain profile: ground elevation with earth curvature, the line-of-sight ray, the Fresnel ellipse, and the worst obstruction marked.

▦ Coverage heatmaps

On any node card, set the remote end's height and gain (what does a 2 m handheld see? a 100 m drone?) and click Coverage heatmap. The map colors everywhere that node can serve, by achievable MCS/throughput — computed over real terrain in every direction, honoring the antenna's beam shape. Recompute after aiming or config changes; run it per-scenario by changing the remote height.

▩ Coverage sim (toolbar) opens the simulation panel. It combines every node's footprint into one overlap map: areas served by one radio show the normal rate colors, two same-band radios show deep blue (redundant — survives any single-node outage), and three or more show purple (extreme coverage — maximum mesh resilience). The legend reports km² per tier, and the figures flow into the exported report.

Two remote platform modes — this is the important choice:

  • 🚙 Terrain-following — the remote hugs the ground at a fixed height (UGV, vehicle, handheld, vessel). The map answers "where can something driving/walking around here connect?" Turn on the near-ground penalty for ground platforms: it applies Doodle Labs' own conservative near-ground propagation curve on top of the terrain physics, taking the worse of the two.
  • 🛩 Flight level — the remote holds a constant altitude ASL, like an aircraft flying level. Drag the altitude slider and the coverage repaints live. Grey areas mean the flight level is inside the terrain there — an aircraft can't occupy that airspace, which is different from "no signal". ⤒ Find lowest workable flight level sweeps altitudes and jumps to the lowest one that still covers the area.

Display metrics let you ask different questions of the same simulation: Data rate (throughput/redundancy tiers), Signal level (dBm), Terrain loss vs free space (the dB the terrain is costing you — the clearest way to see shadows), Link margin, and Minimum altitude to connect.

Minimum altitude to connect colors every point by the lowest height above the ground beneath it at which a link closes. If part of the area also drops out again higher up, the legend warns you: with a narrow-vertical-beam antenna (a high-gain collinear omni, or a downtilted sector) those locations have an altitude window, not a floor — climbing is not always better.

🛣 Mission routes

🛣 Mission route analyses a path rather than an area. Add waypoints by clicking the map, or paste coordinates (decimal degrees, or DMS like 40°01'30"N 105°16'12"W, one per line — the parser skips anything it can't read and tells you). Waypoints are draggable, and the analysis re-runs when you move one.

Configure the vehicle radio separately from the fixed infrastructure: platform, radio, band, channel width, its own antenna from the catalog, and either a height above ground (terrain-following) or a flight level. Every position along the route is then tested against every radio on the map that shares the vehicle's band — radios on other bands are excluded and counted, because they cannot serve it.

You get the achievable bandwidth along the route, the percentage of the route that meets your required data rate, the longest continuous dropout in metres, the number of handovers, and the serving structure — which radio carries which share of the route. The chart shows bandwidth over terrain with the vehicle's path, dropouts shaded red, handover ticks, and a colour strip identifying the serving radio at every point. It all goes into the exported report.

🧭 Extending existing infrastructure

Already have sites deployed (or saved)? In the Plan Advisor choose Extend existing infrastructure: pick your anchor node, pick the target on the map, and say what platform needs service. The advisor evaluates the true terrain path using your node's actual configuration — including how it's currently aimed — and recommends the least-change fix: a remote kit alone if it works, a re-aim, a taller mast, amplification, or a relay site it locates along the profile (with coordinates and mast height) when a single hop can't close. Every option includes a full install spec: boresight bearing, heights, cable class and length.

📄 Deliverables

  • 💾 Save / 📂 Load — layouts save as portable .json files (one per customer engagement); the browser also auto-saves as you work.
  • 📄 Report — exports a customer-ready design document: objective, deployment map, equipment & placement table, per-link terrain analysis with profile charts, and minimum UAV altitudes for Fresnel clearance. Opens anywhere, prints to PDF.

🔬 The math (and its honesty)

Link budgets use the official Doodle Labs methodology — conservative per-MCS sensitivities and transmit powers matching the Doodle Labs Range Estimation Tool exactly, with a 10 dB fade margin. On top of that, this tool adds what a distance-only calculator can't: real elevation profiles (30 m SRTM data), 4/3-earth curvature, the 60% first-Fresnel-zone clearance criterion, ITU-R P.526 knife-edge diffraction, antenna radiation patterns, and frequency-dependent cable loss. Predictions are planning-grade: field-verify critical links, and remember interference, vegetation, and installation quality are not modeled.

💡 Tips

  • Rename nodes (click the name) before exporting — customers see those labels.
  • Keyboard: N = add node, L = link mode.
  • Drag nodes to explore placement — everything recomputes live.
  • Narrower channels (3/5 MHz) buy several dB of sensitivity: when range beats throughput, narrow the channel.
  • For vessels and UGVs, small height changes matter enormously — the first few meters clear the worst of the Fresnel zone.

Step-by-step walkthroughs of the most common jobs. Each takes 2–5 minutes.

🛩 Tutorial 1: Plan a UAV video downlink
  1. Click 🎯 Plan Advisor (mode: New system).
  2. Mission: Ground station ⟷ UAV. Range: your operating radius (e.g. 10 km). Throughput preset: Telemetry + HD video (5).
  3. Click Recommend configurations and read the ranked cards — the 🏆 pick is the least exotic build that closes.
  4. Pan the map to your actual flying site, then click Apply to map.
  5. Rename the nodes (e.g. "GCS", "Bird 1"), drag the GCS to its true launch point, and set the UAV node to your real operating altitude.
  6. The link recomputes over real terrain. Green = fly. If it turns amber/red, open the terrain profile to see exactly which ridge is in the way.
  7. Finish with 📄 Report — it includes the minimum safe altitude for full Fresnel clearance.
📶 Tutorial 2: Design a point-to-point backhaul by hand
  1. + Add Node → click site A. Again for site B.
  2. On each node: platform Fixed mast, set the real mast height, pick a radio + band, and choose a directional antenna (dish/sector) from the catalog.
  3. Blue beam wedges appear — drag each white handle so the beams face each other (or type exact bearings in the Azimuth field).
  4. Set cable type (MC400/MC600) and length — loss computes automatically.
  5. ⟋ Link Nodes → click both nodes. Check the margin in the link card; +10 dB or better is a solid deployment.
  6. Click the link to open the profile — confirm the Fresnel ellipse clears the terrain. If not, raise a mast and watch it clear.
🗺 Tutorial 3: Map handheld coverage around a base
  1. Place a node at the base location; configure its real antenna and height.
  2. In the node card, set Remote ant. (m / dBi) to the handheld's reality: 1.5 m and 3 dBi.
  3. Click ▦ Coverage heatmap. Colors show the best data rate a handheld gets at every spot; the legend explains each band.
  4. Now change remote height to 100 m and recompute — that's the same base serving a drone. The difference is the terrain talking.
  5. Drag the base node to candidate sites and recompute to compare placements.
🚙 Tutorial 3b: Simulate RF loss across terrain for a UGV
  1. Place your ground stations and configure their real antennas and mast heights.
  2. Open ▩ Coverage sim and choose mode 🚙 Terrain-following.
  3. Set height above ground to the vehicle's antenna height (1–2 m for a UGV) and tick near-ground penalty.
  4. Set Display to Terrain loss vs free space (dB). Now the map shows exactly what the terrain costs you: green is a clear path, deep red is a shadow.
  5. Switch Display to Data rate to see the achievable throughput, and to Link margin to find the marginal fringe you should not promise.
  6. Drag a station to a candidate hilltop — the shadows redraw, and you can see which placement fills the valley.
🛩 Tutorial 3c: Find the altitude a UAS needs over your network
  1. Build the ground network (this works with one node or a whole mesh).
  2. Open ▩ Coverage sim, scope ▩ Whole mesh, mode 🛩 Flight level.
  3. Drag the altitude slider. Watch the grey "inside terrain" zones shrink and the purple redundancy zones grow as you climb — that's the terrain releasing its grip.
  4. Click ⤒ Find lowest workable flight level to get the lowest altitude that still covers the area, with the terrain range for context.
  5. Switch Display to Minimum altitude to connect for the climb-height map — and read the warning if part of the area has an altitude window rather than a floor.
  6. Export 📄 Report: the coverage section states the mode and altitude, and the per-link section gives each UAV node's minimum altitude for Fresnel clearance.
🧭 Tutorial 4: Extend an existing network to a new site
  1. Load (📂) or build your current infrastructure on the map.
  2. 🎯 Plan Advisor → mode Extend existing infrastructure.
  3. Pick the anchor node, the platform to serve, and 📍 Pick on map for the target location.
  4. Read the options — least-change first: sometimes it's just a remote kit, sometimes "re-aim your sector 40°", sometimes a relay with exact coordinates.
  5. Apply to map places (and aims) everything; verify the new links are green.
🛣 Tutorial 4b: Prove a patrol route or flight path stays connected
  1. Place and configure the fixed radios first — the route is tested against them.
  2. Click 🛣 Mission route. Either click waypoints along the road/track, or open ⌨ Enter coordinates and paste the customer's waypoint list.
  3. Set the vehicle up: platform (UGV, vehicle, vessel, UAV), radio and band (it must share a band with the ground radios), its antenna, and height above ground — or switch to 🛩 Flight level for an aircraft.
  4. Set Required bandwidth to what the mission actually needs, then Analyse route.
  5. Read the three numbers that matter to a customer: % of route with a link, % at or above the required rate, and longest dropout in metres. Then check the serving structure — if one radio carries 90% of the route, that is a single point of failure worth quoting a second radio against.
  6. Drag a waypoint or a ground radio and it re-analyses immediately, so you can close the gaps interactively.
📡 Tutorial 5: Model a customer's non-standard antenna
  1. In the node's Antenna dropdown choose Other (specify custom)…
  2. Name it, pick the closest type (defaults sensible beamwidths), and enter the gain — or for a dish, just enter the diameter and let it estimate gain/beamwidth.
  3. It behaves like any catalog antenna: beam wedge, aiming, pattern math, reports.
  4. If it's real hardware with a referenceable model number, hit ☁ Share to antenna library so the whole team can reuse it.
📄 Tutorial 6: Produce a customer deliverable
  1. Rename every node to customer-meaningful labels ("HQ Mast", "Patrol Vessel").
  2. Confirm heights, antennas, aiming, and cable specs are the real quote.
  3. 💾 Save the layout .json into the customer's folder — that's your editable source.
  4. 📄 Report → enter a one-line objective → you get a self-contained document with the map, equipment table, per-link terrain analysis, and minimum UAV altitudes. Print to PDF or send as-is.
Why doesn't my link close? The distance seems short.

Nine times out of ten it's height, not distance. Near-ground antennas (1–2 m) graze the terrain, so the Fresnel zone is buried and diffraction eats the budget. Open the terrain profile and look at the ellipse. Fixes in order of cheapness: raise the antennas, narrow the channel (3/5 MHz buys several dB), add gain, add a BDA, add a relay.

The datasheet says 100 km — why does the tool show much less?

The 100 km figures are field demonstrations with high-gain directional antennas, altitude, and clear line of sight. This tool uses Doodle Labs' conservative sales-engineering numbers (same as the official Range Estimation Tool) plus a 10 dB fade margin, so what it promises survives contact with reality. Give it the same dish + altitude and it will show you those big numbers too.

What is MCS and why do I care?

MCS (Modulation and Coding Scheme) is the radio's gearbox: MCS 0 is low gear — slow (≈5 Mbps at 20 MHz) but usable on a whisper of signal; MCS 15 is top gear — ≈83 Mbps but needs ~20 dB more signal. MCS 8–15 use both antennas to carry two data streams (needs 2×2 MIMO both ends). The tool always shows the best MCS your margin supports; the mesh adapts rate automatically in the field.

Why does 900 MHz reach further than 5.8 GHz with the same setup?

Free-space loss grows with frequency (+16 dB from 915 to 5800 MHz). Lower bands also diffract around obstacles better. The trade: less spectrum, narrower channels, less throughput, and bigger antennas. That's why band selection is a mission decision, and why the Plan Advisor searches across bands.

Why does narrowing the channel increase range?

Halving bandwidth halves the noise the receiver ingests: −3 dB noise floor → +3 dB sensitivity. 20 MHz → 5 MHz buys ~6 dB, roughly doubling FSPL range — at proportionally less throughput. When the mission is telemetry, narrow channels are free range.

What do ★, ☁ and unmarked antennas mean?

★ = on Doodle Labs' official Recommended Antenna Matrix. Unmarked = third-party antennas verified against manufacturer datasheets during this tool's catalog build. ☁ = community-submitted by users of this tool (validated for plausibility but not datasheet-verified — treat accordingly).

Can a 2.4 GHz node link to a 5.8 GHz node?

No — both ends of a link must be on the same band and channel to communicate. The tool computes the link at Node A's frequency; keep both nodes' bands matched (the Plan Advisor always does). The exception is the Mesh Rider Dual Radio, which runs two bands simultaneously as separate links.

How do I model an amplifier (BDA)?

Enter its effective gain in the node's BDA gain field (e.g. Mesh Rider Boost ≈ +13 dB TX in C-band; Triad units vary by band). The advisor will also suggest amplification automatically when a path needs it. Watch antenna power ratings — some antennas (e.g. certain 5 W-rated sectors) can't take a 20 W BDA.

Flight level vs height above ground — which should I use for a drone?

Both, for different questions. A drone flying level over varying terrain holds a constant altitude ASL (what a barometric altimeter reads), so the Flight level mode is the honest model of level flight: over a valley the aircraft is high above ground, over a ridge it is close, and the link changes accordingly. Height above ground (terrain-following) answers the regulatory/practical question — small-UAS ceilings such as 400 ft are AGL-referenced, and the Minimum altitude to connect map is deliberately solved in AGL because "climb to X m above the ground" is an instruction a pilot can follow. Use flight level to plan a level mission, AGL to plan a climb.

Why does part of my coverage map turn grey at low flight levels?

Grey means the flight level you selected is below the ground at that point — the terrain is higher than your altitude, so no aircraft can be there. That is deliberately distinguished from "no signal" (left clear). Raise the altitude and the grey shrinks; the exported report also quotes the below-terrain area in km².

Is flying higher always better for the link?

No, and the tool will tell you when it isn't. Climbing clears terrain, but it also moves the aircraft up through the vertical pattern of the ground antenna. A high-gain collinear omni concentrates its energy near the horizon, so a drone that climbs too high flies out of the beam — the classic "cone of silence" overhead. In our testing, a 12° vertical beamwidth left about 38% of the area with an altitude window instead of a simple floor, and a 6° beamwidth about 82%. When the minimum-altitude map detects this, it warns you, and the report states the affected percentage. Plan the altitude band, not just a minimum.

What is the "near-ground penalty" and when should I turn it on?

At 1–2 m above ground the terrain physics alone is optimistic: 30 m elevation data cannot see the fences, ditches, hedgerows and vehicles that actually block a ground platform, and ground reflection makes the real path worse than a clean knife-edge model. The near-ground penalty applies Doodle Labs' own empirical near-ground curve and takes the worse of it and the terrain physics. Turn it on for UGVs, vehicles and handhelds; leave it off for masts, aircraft and vessels. It roughly halved the predicted ground coverage in our test area — that is the point.

How accurate is the terrain data? Are buildings included?

Elevation comes from ~30 m resolution global data (SRTM-derived) — excellent for hills and mountains, blind to buildings, trees, and anything smaller than ~30 m. In cities or forests, treat clear-LOS results as optimistic and budget extra margin. Building-aware modeling is on the roadmap.

Does it model interference, rain, or multipath?

Not explicitly — that's what the 10 dB fade margin is for. In congested RF environments (2.4 GHz in cities) or over water (strong multipath), aim for +15–20 dB margin instead, and prefer the quieter bands.

Who can see my layouts?

Layouts live in your browser and in .json files you save — nothing is uploaded. The one shared thing is antenna submissions via "Share to antenna library", which are visible to all users by design.

Are these predictions guaranteed?

They're planning-grade engineering estimates on conservative assumptions — good enough to design against, not a warranty. Field-verify critical links before committing hardware, especially near-ground, maritime, and urban paths.
